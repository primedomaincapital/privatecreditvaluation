Private credit market size, valuation and NAV, by the numbers

Private credit assets under management, registered-fund holdings, Level 3 shares, secondary-market volume, default rates, and redemption queues, from published sources.

Two different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures the size of the market: assets under management, fundraising, and the share of corporate lending that has moved outside the banks. The second kind measures the price of the market: what share of it sits in assets with no observable price, how often the marks are tested against real transactions, and what happens when investors ask for their money back. The first kind tells a reader how big the valuation job has become. The second tells a reader why the job is hard.

What the latest numbers show is a market that keeps growing and a pricing discipline that is catching up late: assets reached $4.1 trillion at the end of 2025 on the broadest count, while more than half of interval fund assets sit in Level 3, secondary volume jumped 42 percent in a year, and redemption queues at the largest non-traded funds stretched to double-digit shares of outstanding stock.

How to read these numbers

Included: the Alternative Credit Council's end-2025 AUM total; the Federal Reserve's narrower U.S. measure; the SEC staff statement's registered-fund holdings, Level 3 shares, and secondary-market volume; direct-lending and LBO-financing shares; Morgan Stanley's and BlackRock's forecasts, labeled as forecasts; Fitch default rates as reported in secondary summaries; and BDC losses, PIK shares, and redemption queues from an independent census of SEC filings. Excluded: any single "market size" headline presented without its definition, because published totals range from about $1.4 trillion to $4.1 trillion depending on what is counted — the definitions are shown beside each figure instead; and any blending of AUM, invested capital, and dry powder, which describe different things.

Forecasts are labeled as forecasts, not actuals. Secondary summaries of Fitch data are labeled as such; the underlying Fitch figures are not reproduced verbatim from a Fitch publication. BDC figures are arithmetic from SEC filings as compiled by the cited census, not from a BDC's own press release.
