Private Credit Valuation: How the Number Gets Built

A private loan has no market price, so the number on it is built — from spreads, comparable deals, calibration, and a model. In 2026, regulators started checking the work.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 15, 2026 · 7 min read

Every quarter, in a room that could belong to any fund administrator in the city, a valuation committee signs off on numbers nobody traded. The loans on the table have no ticker, no closing price, no bid. Somebody has to say what each one is worth, write the number down, and defend it to an auditor. That number — built, not found — is the whole business of private credit valuation.

The build starts with a rule, not a guess. Under ASC 820, a private loan is a Level 3 asset: no observable market inputs, valued on significant unobservable inputs and management judgment. At the end of 2025, 54% of interval fund assets and 44% of tender offer fund assets sat in Level 3, with a further 21% and 42% respectively valued using net asset value as a practical expedient, according to the SEC staff statement of September 28, 2026. The methods are familiar — discounted cash flow, market multiples, comparable transactions, broker quotes where any exist — but the discipline is in the calibration: the price paid at entry anchors later marks, and every later mark has to be reconciled to it. The SEC’s statement made that calibration mechanics explicit, and a close reading with a worked example shows how an entry price disciplines a mark years after the deal closed.

The job keeps getting bigger. Global private credit assets under management reached $4.1 trillion at the end of 2025, up 17% for a second consecutive year, on the Alternative Credit Council’s count with Houlihan Lokey. The Federal Reserve’s narrower measure puts US private credit loans at about $1.4 trillion — roughly 10% of the debt of American nonfinancial companies and around a third of below-investment-grade debt excluding bank loans. The Investment Company Institute’s measure, cited by the SEC, runs from $357 billion in 2010 to over $1.7 trillion by June 2025. Private credit inside registered fund portfolios reached roughly $270 billion at the end of 2025, up nearly 60% since 2020. Direct lending is now 56% of the asset class, up from 13% in 2010, and private debt supplied 77% of leveraged buyout financing globally in 2024. Every one of those dollars needs a number attached to it, four times a year, by somebody.

The test of the number is the secondary market, and it is growing fast enough to matter. Secondary-market volume reached approximately $220 billion in 2025, up from about $156 billion in 2024 — real transaction prices against which marks can now be checked. The checks are finding gaps. Curia Global, a pharmaceutical contract manufacturer, is carried at 0.09 by Blackstone’s private credit fund, 0.82 by Apollo’s non-traded fund, and 0.973 by Athene. The Truist loan sits at 0.47 in Blue Owl’s flagship fund, 0.95 in its own OCIC sleeve, 0.97 at Goldman Sachs, and 1.24 at Apollo — above what Apollo paid. Across 25 funds and 36,732 loan rows, 88 shared borrowers are marked 10 points or more apart by different holders. Ten points on a $200 million position is $20 million of net asset value.

Timing explains part of the spread, and it is the part most often missed. Insurer marks are annual, struck at December 31, 2025; BDC marks are quarterly 2026 filings — months pass between them, and credit moves in between. Quarterly valuations are the unit of the business: funds mark every quarter, auditors test every year, and the gap between the two is where the questions live. The direction of travel is toward more frequent marks. Kroll and StepStone build loan-level private credit benchmarks from more than 18,000 deals, updated weekly, and Kroll’s daily valuation platform serves funds moving beyond quarterly marks. The quarter is still the reporting unit. It is no longer the only measurement.

That is the question behind the other query this article answers: private credit valuation software. It exists, and the serious products fall into three groups. First, the valuation firms’ own platforms: Kroll’s daily valuation infrastructure, and Stout’s Drivr, the AI-powered portfolio intelligence platform behind Stout’s portfolio valuation and monitoring work. Second, fund platforms that run valuations across strategies: 73 Strings, which extracts and normalizes portfolio company data and runs valuation and monitoring across private equity, private credit, venture capital, and infrastructure on one audit trail. Third, loan-level analytics engines: Vichara’s vLoans, which takes raw loan tape to priced, monitored portfolios with loan-level cashflows, yield, IRR, and scenario analysis; and Milliman’s M-PIRe, a mortgage valuation and securitization platform with loan-level performance models. The honest summary is that software computes and documents — it does not sign. No platform produces a defensible mark on its own. Calibration, judgment, and an auditor willing to challenge the number still sit on top, and any vendor that implies otherwise is selling the audit trail as the audit.

The uncomfortable truth is that dispersion is not, by itself, proof of mispricing. Cost bases differ. Dates differ. Most funds cluster at the peer median — Apollo’s non-traded fund sits at exactly zero against the median on 141 shared names, Blackstone’s BCRED at zero on 215. BCRED’s weighted average mark of 95.4 sits in line with the leveraged loan index, and its bottom 5% of private debt investments, marked at an average of 63.4, are first-lien senior secured positions with an average loan-to-value of 39% at underwrite. The stress is real — 125 business development companies booked $7.00 billion of net realized and unrealized losses in the first half of 2026, 65.6% of their $10.67 billion of net investment income, and Fitch’s privately monitored default rate ran 9.2% in 2025 — but nobody is reporting a default wave. They are reporting a markdown and exit squeeze, with redemption requests at the largest non-traded funds running from 10% to nearly 17% of shares. The market is pricing one loan at a time, in public, with the disagreements visible to anyone who reads the filings.

That visibility is what changed. An investor’s job is no longer to read the NAV. It is to ask for the dispersion — how far apart the holders of the same loans sit, and why — to ask how the marks are calibrated to entry prices, and how often they are tested against secondary trades. The firms that can show that work, line by line, will keep the mandates. The ones that can only show the number will find the questions getting sharper every quarter, because the regulators who asked them in September 2026 have not stopped asking.
