Nine Cents Against Ninety-Seven

Three funds hold the same loan. One marks it at nine cents on the dollar. Another at ninety-seven. The NAV is a committee number — and 2026 is the year the committee got audited.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Take one loan, held by three funds, and ask each what it is worth. Curia Global, a pharmaceutical contract manufacturer, is carried by Blackstone’s Private Credit Fund at 0.09 — nine cents on the dollar of what the fund paid. Apollo’s non-traded fund carries the same loan at 0.82. Athene, the annuity insurer, filed its statutory statement carrying it at 0.973, one grade above the regulator’s worst category. Nine cents against ninety-seven cents. Same borrower, same loan, an 88-cent spread between two audited numbers, both signed and filed.

Curia is the extreme, but it is not an outlier in kind. An analysis of every Schedule of Investments filed this cycle — 25 funds, 36,732 loan rows — found 1,489 companies whose debt is held by more than one fund. On 88 of those companies, the funds disagree about what the same loan is worth by 10 points or more. Ten points is not a rounding difference: on a $200 million position it is $20 million of net asset value, the number the fund reports to its investors as the value of their money. The Truist loan shows the same pattern inside a single manager: Blue Owl’s flagship fund carries it at 0.47, its own OCIC sleeve at 0.95, Goldman Sachs at 0.97, Apollo at 1.24. Apollo carries the loan above what it paid. Blue Owl’s flagship says it is worth barely a third of that.

2026 is the year the disagreement stopped being an academic footnote and became a profit-and-loss event. In the six months to June 30, 2026, 125 business development companies booked $7.00 billion of net realized and unrealized losses — 65.6% of their $10.67 billion of net investment income, and up from $1.64 billion in the first half of 2025 and $0.56 billion in 2024. At Blackstone’s BCRED, $1.95 billion of losses nearly wiped out $2.00 billion of net investment income, leaving a net gain from operations of $44.8 million. The Federal Reserve’s May 2026 Financial Stability Report puts U.S. private credit at about $1.4 trillion, roughly 10% of the debt of American nonfinancial companies and around one-third of below-investment-grade debt excluding bank loans. A Reuters analysis of filings from 44 U.S. BDCs found combined investments at a fair value of $92.88 billion against a reported cost of $95.19 billion at June 30 — the market, such as it is, is now marking the book below what it paid.

Investors have noticed. Requests to sell at BCRED rose from 1.46% of shares in December 2024 to 10.30% in the offer that closed in May 2026, and the fund paid 48.5% of them; a September 2026 report put third-quarter requests at about 10% of outstanding shares, against a program that permits repurchases of only 5% of net asset value. The pattern repeats across the large non-traded funds: Apollo Debt Solutions went from 0.77% of shares requesting redemption in December 2024 to 16.80% in June 2026, HPS Corporate Lending from 1.40% to 13.30%, Ares Strategic Income from 0.51% to 14.54%. Non-accruals at six large non-traded BDCs ran from 0.1% to 2.2% of cost, and payment-in-kind interest — interest paid in more debt rather than cash — was 6.09% of income at the same funds. Nobody is reporting a default wave. They are reporting a markdown and exit squeeze, and the queue to leave is growing faster than the door.

The honest version of the story is less alarming than the Curia spread suggests, and more important. Marks disagree partly because cost bases differ and dates differ: insurer marks are annual, struck at December 31, 2025, while BDC marks are quarterly 2026 filings, so time passes between them. Most funds cluster at the peer median — Apollo’s non-traded fund sits at exactly zero against the median on 141 shared names, Blackstone’s BCRED at zero on 215. BCRED’s weighted average mark of 95.4 sits in line with the leveraged loan index, and its bottom 5% of private debt investments, marked at an average of 63.4, are first-lien senior secured positions with an average loan-to-value of 39% at underwrite. The system is not hiding a crisis. It is pricing one loan at a time, in public, with the disagreements visible to anyone who reads the filings.

That visibility is the point, and it is why valuation has become the product. The SEC’s September 2026 staff statement on fair value measurement for private assets, the joint statement that followed it, and the daily-valuation benchmarks now sold by Kroll and StepStone all answer the same question the Curia spread asks: if three funds can hold the same loan at 0.09, 0.82, and 0.973, what does a NAV actually mean? The answer 2026 is converging on is that a NAV is a committee number, and a committee number is only as good as the calibration behind it. The investor’s job is no longer to read the NAV. It is to ask for the dispersion — how far apart the holders of the same loans sit, and why.
