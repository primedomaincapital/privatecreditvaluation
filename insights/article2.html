Daily Marks Are Coming for a Quarterly Market

Private credit was built on quarterly marks. Investors, regulators, and redemption windows are now pulling it toward daily pricing.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

In the spring of 2026, an investor in a large non-traded private credit fund asked to take some money out. The request was not unusual — these funds promise periodic redemptions — but the number attached to it was. Requests to sell at Blackstone's Private Credit Fund had climbed from 1.46 percent of shares in December 2024 to 10.30 percent in the offer that closed in May 2026, and the fund paid less than half of them. The investor's money was priced at a NAV struck weeks earlier, from marks struck a quarter earlier, on loans that had not traded in years. The queue was the market's way of saying the price and the moment had come apart.

That gap is the story of private credit valuation in 2026. The asset class was designed around patience: loans negotiated one-to-one, held for years, marked quarterly by the manager's own team, reported to investors who had agreed not to ask for their money back very often. The design worked while the money stayed put. It is now under strain from three directions at once — investors who want out, regulators who want evidence, and competitors who can price faster.

The redemption numbers are the sharpest evidence. At Blackstone's fund, the queue went from 1.46 percent to 10.30 percent in seventeen months, with 48.5 percent of requests paid. The pattern repeated across the largest non-traded business development companies: Apollo Debt Solutions saw requests rise from 0.77 percent of shares in December 2024 to 16.80 percent by June 2026; HPS Corporate Lending went from 1.40 percent to 13.30 percent; Ares Strategic Income from 0.51 percent to 14.54 percent. All of them prorated in 2026, paying between 29.8 and 54.0 percent of each request. The Federal Reserve noticed the same pressure in its May 2026 Financial Stability Report, recording increased redemption requests at some semi-liquid private credit vehicles in late 2025 and early 2026 — manageable at the time, but no longer theoretical.

A queue is, in the end, a valuation dispute wearing a different costume. When investors believe the NAV is stale — too high relative to what the loans would fetch — the rational move is to redeem at the stale price before the mark catches up. When they believe it is too low, they stay. A redemption queue that keeps growing is the market voting that the number on the statement is not the number a buyer would pay. And every proration teaches the next investor to ask earlier, which makes the queue longer, which makes the mark look staler. The circle only breaks when the mark itself becomes credible enough that nobody needs to race it.

Regulators are pushing from the other side. Australia's securities regulator, ASIC, published two reports on the sector in 2025: Report 814, an independent review released in September, found valuations often infrequent, managed internally, or lacking independent oversight; Report 820, released in November, set out surveillance of 28 retail and wholesale private credit funds conducted between October 2024 and August 2025 and found poor valuation practices that affected entry and exit prices, reported performance, and fees. Some funds had no valuation policy at all, or an inadequate one. ASIC has made poor practice in private credit an enforcement priority for 2026. The Financial Stability Board's first dedicated report on private credit vulnerabilities, published May 6, 2026, put the global market at $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion and warned that procyclical redemption pressures could force asset sales — the same mechanism the BDC queues were already demonstrating.

The industry's answer, interestingly, is not to defend quarterly marks. It is to build the machinery to price faster. Kroll and StepStone launched their private credit benchmarks in September 2025, built on loan-level data from more than 15,000 deals and updated weekly — a deliberate break from fund-level aggregates and modeled estimates. The benchmarks now cover more than 18,000 loan-level deals, with data weekly and a monthly market report; the September 2026 edition noted spreads and yields continuing to compress year over year while interest coverage firmed across segments. Kroll's pitch is explicit: private credit is moving to daily valuations, and the tools to value it daily, systematically, and defensibly now exist. The argument is that observable new-issue pricing data is the foundation daily marks were missing until now.

There is a genuine debate underneath the technology, and it deserves a fair hearing. Daily marks are not obviously better marks. A loan that does not trade has no daily price to observe; a daily mark is still a model output, just produced more often. Critics of the rush to frequency point out that quarterly marks were a feature, not a bug — they kept long-term capital from being whipsawed by public-market sentiment, and they let managers underwrite to maturity rather than to the next redemption window. Push the frequency too far, and you import the volatility private credit was built to escape, while spending a fortune on infrastructure to produce numbers nobody asked for. The Alternative Credit Council's 2026 report leans this way: its survey found 59 percent of corporate lending funds operate with leverage, fund borrowing of about $399 billion — roughly 25 percent of net assets — and yet the authors describe pressures as concentrated rather than systemic, with about one-sixth of funds reporting non-accrual rates above 5 percent.

But the counterargument is losing ground to arithmetic. The market has doubled and doubled again — from about $357 billion in 2010 to over $1.7 trillion by June 2025 on the Investment Company Institute's measure, and to $4.1 trillion globally at the end of 2025 on the Alternative Credit Council's broader count, up 17 percent for a second straight year. Fundraising in the first half of 2026 reached its highest level in five years. Morgan Stanley expects $5 trillion by 2029; BlackRock projects $4.5 trillion by 2030. A $4 trillion market cannot run its pricing on a quarterly ritual performed by the same team that originated the loans. The investors now arriving — insurers, who named private debt their top increased allocation at 41 percent of respondents in BlackRock's 15th annual Global Insurance Survey of 463 executives overseeing more than $24 trillion — will not accept marks they cannot test.

What comes next is a split. The largest managers will build or buy daily pricing infrastructure and treat it as a competitive advantage, the way the largest public-market managers treat execution. Smaller managers will outsource it to administrators and valuation firms. Either way, the work moves from the manager's desk to an independent process with an audit trail — and the firms that can run that process, with real transaction data behind it, become the market's new infrastructure. Quarterly marks will not disappear. They will stop being the whole story.
