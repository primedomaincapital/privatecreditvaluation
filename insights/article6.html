The Valuation Agent’s Fee: What Independent Pricing Actually Costs a Fund

A fund pays its valuation agent a few thousand dollars a loan — or six figures for a hard one. The invoice is small. The number it buys is the fund’s net asset value.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The engagement letter arrives every January, and every January the chief financial officer reads it twice. The first read is the fee schedule. The second read is the scope: how many loans, how many quarters, what counts as a complex credit, what happens when the agent disagrees with the fund. The number at the bottom is rarely the largest line in the fund’s budget. It is the line the auditor asks about first.

What the fund is buying is not a number. It is the right to say the number came from somewhere else. A private loan has no market price, so the mark is built from spreads, comparable deals, calibration to the entry price, and a model — and under ASC 820 it is a Level 3 asset, valued on significant unobservable inputs and management judgment. An independent valuation agent does the building at arm’s length, documents the method, and signs a report the auditor can test. Without that signature, the fund is marking its own homework, and every auditor, regulator, and limited partner in 2026 knows it.

The price of that signature has a long paper trail. The clearest public benchmark is an engagement letter Duff & Phelps filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission in 2013, pricing independent valuation of individual loans at $1,500 to $2,000 per loan, with quarterly updates at 25% of the initial fee. The figure is thirteen years old and it shows: it predates the market’s growth, the SEC’s 2026 statement on fair value, and the dispersion evidence that made independent marks a governance question rather than a clerical one. It is still the only per-loan price in the public record, and it still anchors the negotiation. A fund with two hundred loans can do the arithmetic in its head: roughly $300,000 to $400,000 for the first full valuation, then a quarter of that each quarter after, before complexity adjustments.

For the harder credits, the pricing moves to a different scale entirely. A 2026 guide from CT Acquisitions puts independent business valuation engagements at $15,000 to $75,000 for mid-market work, and $100,000 to $500,000 for complex or litigation-grade assignments. A distressed loan with a broken covenant, a payment-in-kind toggle, and a borrower in restructuring is not a $2,000 loan. It is a valuation engagement in its own right, with its own model, its own comparable set, and its own argument with the auditor. The fee follows the argument.

The market for this work has grown with the asset class it serves. Global private credit assets under management reached $4.1 trillion at the end of 2025, up 17% for a second consecutive year, on the Alternative Credit Council’s count with Houlihan Lokey. The Federal Reserve’s narrower measure puts United States private credit loans at about $1.4 trillion. There are more than 170 business development companies holding $556.8 billion at fair value across some 10,600 companies, according to Houlihan Lokey’s BDC Monitor for the fall of 2026. Every one of those positions needs a number four times a year, and the firms that supply the numbers — Kroll, Houlihan Lokey, Lincoln International, Stout, Murray Devine — have built the infrastructure to do it at scale: Kroll and StepStone’s loan-level benchmarks now draw on more than 18,000 deals, updated weekly, and Kroll runs a daily valuation platform for funds moving beyond quarterly marks.

The fees are rising because the scrutiny is rising. The SEC’s staff statement of September 28, 2026, made calibration mechanics explicit — the entry price disciplines later marks, and the reconciliation has to be shown. The dispersion evidence is now public and specific: Curia Global, a pharmaceutical contract manufacturer, is carried at 0.09 by Blackstone’s private credit fund, 0.82 by Apollo’s non-traded fund, and 0.973 by Athene. Across 25 funds and 36,732 loan rows, 88 shared borrowers are marked 10 points or more apart by different holders, according to Event Horizon IQ. When two holders of the same loan disagree by 88 points, the question is no longer whether the valuation agent was expensive. It is whether the fund can explain why it did not hire one.

What the agent delivers is not a number on a page. It is a report: the methodology chosen and why, the comparable transactions and the adjustments made to each, the calibration table showing how the mark reconciles to the entry price, and the sensitivity analysis showing how far the number moves when the key assumptions move. The auditor’s job is to re-perform that work — to take the agent’s workbook, change the discount rate, and see whether the answer still holds. A report that cannot be re-performed is a press release with a signature, and the auditors who test private credit marks in 2026 know the difference. The fee buys the workbook as much as the number, and the workbook is what the LP never sees and the auditor always asks for.

Frequency is the other half of the fee question. The traditional engagement is an annual full valuation with quarterly updates at a fraction of the initial fee — the Duff & Phelps letter put the update at 25% — but the market is moving toward more frequent marks, and the fee schedule is moving with it. Kroll’s daily valuation platform serves funds that mark beyond the quarter, and Kroll and StepStone’s benchmarks update weekly from more than 18,000 deals. A fund that pays for an annual valuation and quarterly updates is buying four looks a year at a portfolio that reprices every day, and the gap between the two is where the redemption risk lives.

The counterargument is worth stating plainly, because it is the one every CFO makes in January: the fee is trivial next to the assets. A $400,000 annual valuation bill on a $2 billion fund is two basis points. The management fee is a hundred times that. The real cost of a bad mark is not the agent’s invoice — it is the markdown. In the first half of 2026, 125 business development companies booked $7.00 billion of net realized and unrealized losses, 65.6% of their $10.67 billion of net investment income. Blackstone’s BCRED saw non-accruals rise from 0.5% at the end of 2024 to 2.4% by March 2026. Ten points on a $200 million position is $20 million of net asset value. Against numbers like those, arguing about $2,000 a loan is arguing about the wrong decimal place.

That argument cuts both ways, and it is the reason the fee negotiation matters more than its size suggests. A cheap valuation agent is cheap for a reason: fewer comparables, a thinner model, less willingness to disagree with the fund. The agent’s value is not in the number it produces — it is in the disagreement it is willing to have, on the record, before the auditor arrives. A fund that hires the cheapest agent has bought the signature and skipped the protection, and the protection is the whole point.

Before signing the next engagement letter, ask four questions. First, is the fee per loan or per portfolio, and what moves a loan into the complex band? Second, what do quarterly updates cost relative to the initial valuation — the 25% figure from the Duff & Phelps letter is the benchmark, and anything far above it deserves a reason. Third, who owns the model: does the agent deliver a number, or a workbook the auditor can re-perform? Fourth, what happens when the agent disagrees with the fund — is the disagreement documented, escalated, and disclosed, or quietly resolved in the fund’s favor? The answers are worth more than the fee. The fee is the smallest number in the room. The NAV it defends is the largest.
