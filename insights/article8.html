The LP’s Question: How to Read a Private Credit Valuation Report

The quarterly report is not the number. It is the audit trail behind the number — and the questions it does not answer are the ones that matter.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The quarterly package arrives at 9 p.m., eighty pages in a PDF, and the limited partner’s analyst has an hour before the investment committee meets in the morning. The net asset value is on page two. The performance is on page three. The rest — the capital account roll-forward, the fee schedule, the portfolio company pages, the valuation policy — is where the actual story lives, and it is the part most readers skip. That is a mistake. The NAV is a conclusion. The report is the evidence. Reading it backwards, from the evidence to the conclusion, is the only way to know whether the number means anything.

Start with the roll-forward, because it is the one page that cannot hide. The Institutional Limited Partners Association’s reporting template lays it out as an accounting identity: ending NAV equals beginning NAV plus contributions, minus distributions, minus the management fee, plus fee offsets, minus internal chargebacks and external partnership expenses, plus investment income, plus realized and unrealized gain, minus the change in accrued carried interest. Every dollar has to appear somewhere, and the total has to reconcile to the fund’s financial statements and the partner capital account statement. If it does not reconcile, stop reading. Nothing else in the package can be trusted until it does.

The template itself changed this year, and the change matters. ILPA released its updated Reporting Template version 2.0 on January 21, 2025, the culmination of its Quarterly Reporting Standards Initiative, and the first delivery under the new standard covers the first quarter of 2026, with data as of March 31, 2026. The new version standardizes the disclosure of fees, expenses, and carried interest in far greater detail than the 2016 template it replaces — offering and syndication costs, placement fees, partner transfers, and a separate schedule of every fee the adviser and its related persons received from portfolio companies, including fees not subject to offset. Funds still in their investment period during the first quarter of 2026, and funds commencing operations on or after January 1, 2026, are expected to use it. Funds already out of their investment period on January 1, 2026, may keep the 2016 version. The first question to ask any manager is which template you are holding, and why.

The reason the standard exists is that the old disclosure was not enough. The Securities and Exchange Commission’s private fund adviser rules would have mandated quarterly statements of similar detail; the Fifth Circuit vacated those rules in June 2024, and ILPA continued the work as an industry-led standard instead. Large institutional investors now ask for the templates in side letters and during due diligence, which makes them a commercial expectation for any manager raising from pensions or endowments — but they remain voluntary, and a manager that does not provide them is telling you something about how it expects to be questioned.

After the roll-forward, read the performance page — and read it for what it omits. The standard metrics are the internal rate of return net of fees and carried interest, since inception and for trailing periods, alongside TVPI, DPI, and RVPI at fund level and by vintage year. The public market equivalent compares the fund against a relevant benchmark. What matters is not the headline IRR but the relationship between DPI and RVPI: distributions against the value still sitting in the portfolio. A fund with a strong TVPI and a DPI near zero has not returned anything yet; it has marked things. In a market where 88 shared borrowers are marked 10 points or more apart by different holders, according to Event Horizon IQ’s September 2026 analysis of 25 funds and 36,732 loan rows, the difference between a realized gain and an unrealized one is the difference between money and opinion.

The portfolio company pages are where the valuation story gets specific, and where the questions get sharp. The template asks for acquisition date, cost, current fair value, trailing and forward EBITDA, revenue, leverage, equity ownership, and management commentary on significant developments. Read the leverage column against the fair value column. A company whose debt-to-EBITDA has risen while its fair value has stayed flat is a mark that has not moved with the credit. Read the commentary for the word that matters most: “unchanged.” A portfolio in which nothing changed in a quarter when the leveraged loan distress ratio rose to 6.1% in July 2026 from 3.4%, and Fitch’s trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record 6.3% through August 2026, is a portfolio whose marks are stale.

The fee schedule is the page most readers skip and the page that most often explains the rest. The new template requires a separate schedule of every fee the adviser and its related persons received from portfolio companies, including fees not subject to offset — transaction fees, monitoring fees, directors’ fees, broken-deal expenses — and the offsets shown in the capital account roll-forward have to tie to it. Read the management fee basis first: is it charged on committed capital or invested capital, and does the basis step down after the investment period? Read the carried interest accrual next, because accrued carry is the line that moves the NAV most sharply in a good quarter and reverses fastest in a bad one, and the template requires a reconciliation of it. A fee that appears in the schedule and not in the roll-forward, or an offset that appears in the roll-forward and not in the schedule, is the same kind of problem as a NAV that does not reconcile: the package is telling two stories, and one of them is wrong.

The counterargument is worth taking seriously, because it is the one managers make and it is partly right: more disclosure does not equal more understanding. Two funds can fill the same template, follow the same standard, and still disagree by 88 points on the same loan — Curia Global, a pharmaceutical contract manufacturer, is carried at 0.09 by Blackstone’s private credit fund, 0.82 by Apollo’s non-traded fund, and 0.973 by Athene. The template standardizes the presentation of the number. It does not standardize the judgment behind it. A perfectly formatted report can still be wrong, and a sloppy one can still be right. The report is evidence, not proof.

That is exactly why the report matters, though. The judgment is visible in the places the template forces it into the open: the unrealized gain line, the accrued carry, the fee offsets, the valuation policy disclosure that must accompany each quarterly package. A manager that changed its valuation policy mid-year has to say so. A manager whose marks moved sharply has to show where they moved, loan by loan, in the unrealized gain. The secondary market now provides a check the template cannot: about $220 billion of secondary volume in 2025, up from about $156 billion in 2024, means real transaction prices exist against which the marks can be tested, and the testing is happening in public.

Five questions, asked every quarter, will tell you more than the NAV ever will. Which template is this, and is it the current one? Does the roll-forward reconcile to the financial statements, to the dollar? What is the ratio of distributions to unrealized value — how much of the return is money, and how much is marks? Which portfolio companies moved, and did the ones that did not move have a reason? And when the valuation policy changed, or a loan went to workout, or a fee appeared in the schedule that was not there last quarter, was the change disclosed before you had to ask? A manager that answers all five, in the report itself, is a manager whose number you can use. A manager that answers none of them is asking you to take the number on trust, and in private credit in 2026, trust is the one asset nobody should hold unhedged.
