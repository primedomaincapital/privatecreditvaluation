The Workout Valuation: What a Distressed Loan Is Worth When the Borrower Misses

A missed payment turns a model into a negotiation. The workout valuation is where a fund’s net asset value meets the borrower’s actual cash.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The call comes on a Monday, and it is short. The borrower missed a payment on Friday. The covenant test failed in the same week. The mark on the loan, struck at the end of last quarter, is already fiction — it assumed a borrower that no longer exists. Somebody has to put a new number on the position before the quarter closes, and the new number cannot come from a spreadsheet that still believes the old story. That is the workout valuation, and it is the hardest number in private credit to get right.

The first thing the workout changes is the question. A performing loan is valued on the cash flows the contract promises: coupon, amortization, maturity. A distressed loan is valued on the cash flows the borrower can actually produce, discounted for the time and the legal cost of getting them. The methods are the same ones the SEC’s September 2026 staff statement describes for Level 3 assets — discounted cash flow, market multiples, comparable transactions — but the inputs are different in kind. The discount rate is no longer a spread over a benchmark. It is a judgment about whether the borrower survives, and at what price.

The scale of that judgment is now visible in the filings. Non-accruals across business development companies stood at 1.9% of portfolio cost in the second quarter of 2026, down from 2.1% in the first quarter, according to Houlihan Lokey’s BDC Monitor for the fall of 2026 — but the implied recovery on those loans fell to 47.2%, from 54.2%, the lowest since the fourth quarter of 2024. The direction matters more than the level: fewer loans are going bad, and the ones that do are worth less. KBRA’s direct lending default rate reached 2.3% on a trailing twelve-month basis, matching its December 2023 high, with a forecast of 3.5% by the end of 2026. Fitch’s trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record 6.3% through August 2026, with healthcare and industrial borrowers at 9.9% and software at 0.6%. The market is not in a default wave. It is in a recovery squeeze, and the squeeze is where the workout valuation earns its keep.

Position in the capital structure decides most of the answer before the analysis starts. First-lien unitranche loans are recovering around 94 cents on the dollar in recent workout experience; second-lien positions are recovering around 82 cents. The 2024 vintage tells the harder story: second-lien recoveries averaged just 38 cents that year. The gap between the two years is the lesson — recovery is not a property of the loan, it is a property of the cycle, the collateral, and the negotiating table. A valuation that quotes a historical average recovery without naming the vintage it came from is not conservative. It is careless.

The most common outcome is not a default at all. It is an amend-and-extend: the lender agrees to push the maturity, loosen the covenant, and keep the borrower alive. Amend-and-extend volume reached $47 billion in the first half of 2026, up from $22 billion in the first half of 2025, and 71% of those deals came with covenant modifications. Each one is a valuation event, because each one rewrites the cash flows the mark was built on. A loan that was worth 97 cents on a five-year maturity with a tight covenant is worth something else on a seven-year maturity with a loose one, and the difference is not a rounding error. It is the price of the borrower’s survival, paid by the lender in the form of a lower mark.

The payment-in-kind toggle is the tell that the workout has already started. Fitch found that payment-in-kind interest and deferrals featured in 47% of the 89 private credit defaults it tracked through August 2026. A borrower that pays its coupon in more debt instead of cash is not servicing the loan; it is capitalizing the shortfall and hoping the exit arrives before the arithmetic does. The mark on a PIK loan has to answer a question the coupon never asks: what is the borrower worth when the debt it owes is larger than the debt it borrowed? Blackstone’s BCRED, the largest non-traded business development company, saw non-accruals rise from 0.5% at the end of 2024 to 2.4% by March 2026, with net unrealized depreciation widening from $717 million to $1,884 million. The numbers are public. The judgment behind them is the workout valuation.

The valuation itself runs on three approaches, and a defensible workout mark uses at least two of them. The income approach discounts the restructured cash flows — the reduced coupon, the extended maturity, the fees the borrower pays for the amendment — at a rate that reflects the borrower’s actual risk, not the rate it borrowed at when times were good. The market approach looks for secondary trades in the same loan or in comparable distressed paper, and in a year when secondary volume reached about $220 billion, those trades increasingly exist. The asset approach values the collateral — the plant, the receivables, the intellectual property — on a liquidation basis, which is the floor below which the loan cannot fall unless the collateral itself is impaired. When the three approaches disagree, the disagreement is the analysis: the income approach says what the borrower can pay, the market approach says what a buyer will pay, and the asset approach says what the lender gets if neither of those happens.

The counterargument deserves a fair hearing, because it is the one the optimists make and it is not foolish: distressed marks are the most honest marks in the portfolio. A performing loan’s value is a model’s opinion about a future that has not happened. A loan in workout is valued against an actual negotiation, an actual buyer, an actual liquidation value — and the discipline of a counterparty who wants to pay less. The 12.3% of borrowers that showed valuation gaps of five points or more in June 2026, up from 9.3%, are not a scandal. They are the market working out, in public, what the models could not agree on in private. The workout does not create the loss. It reveals it.

That is true, and it is also incomplete, because the revelation arrives on the lender’s timetable, not the borrower’s. The leveraged loan distress ratio rose to 6.1% in July 2026 from 3.4%, and the secondary market is now large enough — about $220 billion of volume in 2025, up from about $156 billion in 2024 — that a fund which will not mark its distressed loans to the workout will find the market marking them anyway, in the price of its own shares. Non-traded funds returned $12.7 billion to investors in the first half of 2026 against $9.7 billion raised. Redemption windows do not wait for the workout to finish.

The practical test for any workout valuation is three questions, asked in order. First, what does the borrower actually generate in cash, after the costs of staying alive — not what the model assumed at underwrite, and not what the sponsor’s forecast promises for next year. Second, what is the position worth in a sale today, to a buyer who knows everything the lender knows — because that is the only number that cannot be argued with. Third, what does the amendment cost, in maturity, covenant, and coupon, and is that cost reflected in the mark or deferred to the next quarter? A valuation that answers all three, with the numbers shown, will survive the auditor, the LP, and the redemption window. A valuation that answers none of them is a performing mark on a loan that stopped performing months ago, and the market will find it eventually.
